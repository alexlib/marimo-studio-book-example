Chapter 2

Drawing with Eigenvectors

A graph has no coordinates — only connections. The eigenvectors of its Laplacian invent coordinates that respect them.

A matrix for a graph

Chapter 1 studied a matrix we chose by hand. This chapter builds one from a graph. Write A for the adjacency matrix — a 1 wherever two nodes are connected — and D for the diagonal matrix of node degrees. Their difference is the graph Laplacian:

What makes this matrix special is its quadratic form. Assign a number xi to every node, and the Laplacian adds up the disagreement across each edge:

An eigenvector with a small eigenvalue is therefore a numbering that barely disagrees across edges — connected nodes get similar values. Use two such eigenvectors as (x, y) coordinates and connected nodes land near each other on the page. That is spectral graph drawing.

Two drawings, one graph

Both panels below draw exactly the same nodes and edges. The left panel throws the nodes down at random. The right panel places node i at the point whose coordinates are the i-th entries of two Laplacian eigenvectors. No force simulation, no iteration — one call to eigh and the structure appears.

Try each graph in the picker above. The cycle unrolls into a true circle, and the grid reproduces itself — the eigenvectors of a grid are products of cosines, so the drawing is a slightly curved lattice.

Read the spectrum

The full set of eigenvalues is a fingerprint of the graph. Small eigenvalues correspond to smooth, global structure; large ones to rapid local oscillation. Your current axes are highlighted.

Chapter 3 turns this observation into an algorithm: if smooth structure lives at the low end of the spectrum, then noise — which is anything but smooth — can be removed by discarding the high end.
